Have you ever tried to explain why something hurt you, only to hear, “You are overreacting,” “That is not what happened,” or, “I do not agree with you”? Maybe the other person was trying to defend themselves. Maybe they thought that if they could prove your interpretation was wrong, the problem would disappear. But when someone is distressed, being corrected is not always what they need first. Often, they need to know that their emotional experience has been heard.

That is where validation comes in. Validation is the act of communicating that another person’s feelings make sense when viewed through their experience. It does not mean that every conclusion they have reached is accurate. It does not mean that you agree with their version of events. It does not mean that you accept blame for something you did not do. And it certainly does not mean that you must surrender your own perspective to keep the peace.

Validation and agreement are different skills. Agreement says, “I see the situation the way you see it.” Validation says, “I can understand why this situation felt this way to you.” That distinction is small in wording, but enormous in practice. It allows two people to hold different interpretations without immediately turning the conversation into a contest.

Imagine that a friend sends you an important message, and you do not reply until the next day. When you finally respond, your friend says, “I felt ignored. I started wondering whether I had done something wrong.” You might know that you were travelling, exhausted, or dealing with a personal emergency. You might be tempted to answer, “I was busy. You know I have a life. You are making this into something it is not.” That response explains your circumstance, but it does not acknowledge your friend’s experience.

A validating response could sound like this: “I can understand why the silence felt like rejection, especially because the message was important to you. I was overwhelmed and did not handle communication well, but I can see how the delay affected you.” Notice what this response does. It recognizes the feeling without confirming the fear that you no longer care. It gives context without using context as a weapon. It opens a door to repair.

Validation begins with listening for the emotional meaning beneath the words. People do not always state their feelings directly. Someone may say, “You never tell me anything,” when the deeper experience is, “I do not feel included.” Someone may say, “Fine, do whatever you want,” when the deeper experience is disappointment or powerlessness. Someone may become angry because anger feels safer than saying, “I am afraid I do not matter to you.”

This does not mean you should psychoanalyze every sentence. You do not need to guess perfectly. You can simply check your understanding. You might say, “It sounds like you felt left out. Is that what you mean?” Or, “Are you hurt because you wanted more notice?” A question like this communicates attention while leaving room for correction.

The first part of a useful validation is presence. Put down the impulse to prepare your defence. Stop composing your counterargument while the other person is speaking. Notice their words, their tone, their pace, and the feeling underneath the message. You do not have to solve the problem immediately. Sometimes the most powerful first response is a calm, “I am listening.”

The second part is reflection. Repeat the central meaning in your own words. You might say, “You were expecting me to support you, and when I changed the subject, it felt like I did not care.” Or, “You were already stressed, and my joke landed as criticism.” Reflection is not parroting. It is showing that you made an effort to understand what the person was trying to communicate.

The third part is recognition. Name why the feeling is understandable in context. You can say, “Given what happened earlier, it makes sense that you became cautious.” You can say, “I can see why that would feel embarrassing.” You can say, “Anyone might feel disappointed after making that effort and receiving no response.” Recognition helps a person feel less alone inside their reaction.

The fourth part is curiosity. Ask what matters most now. “Do you want me to listen, help you think through a solution, or apologise for my part?” This question is useful because people often want different kinds of support. One person may want comfort. Another may want practical action. A third may simply want a witness while they organise their thoughts.

The fifth part is your own perspective. Once the emotion has been acknowledged, you can offer a different view. The order matters. If you lead with contradiction, the other person may hear your explanation as a refusal to care. If you validate first, there is a greater chance that both of you can think clearly. You might say, “I understand why you felt excluded. I also want to explain what was happening from my side, because I do not want you to think the silence was intentional.”

This is not a trick for getting someone to agree with you. It is a way of lowering the emotional temperature so that truth can be discussed without humiliation. You can validate and disagree in the same conversation. “I understand why you felt betrayed when I cancelled. I do not agree that I cancelled because I do not value you.” Both statements can be true. The feeling deserves care. The conclusion deserves examination.

It is also possible to validate without accepting responsibility for something that is not yours. Suppose someone says, “You made me feel worthless.” You could respond, “I am sorry that my words left you feeling worthless. That was not what I wanted, and I want to understand which part hurt.” This acknowledges impact without declaring that you control the person’s entire emotional state. You can take responsibility for your behavior while remembering that each adult is also responsible for their interpretations, choices, and healing.

Validation should never be used to excuse harmful conduct. If someone says they are angry, you can validate the anger without accepting threats or intimidation. “I hear that you are furious, and I am willing to discuss what happened. I will not stay in a conversation where I am being threatened.” If someone is jealous, you can understand the fear without handing over your privacy or freedom. If someone is grieving, you can honour their pain without promising something impossible. Compassion does not require the removal of boundaries.

There are several common responses that sound supportive but miss the point. “At least it was not worse” may be intended to create perspective, but it can make pain feel illegitimate. “Do not think about it” asks someone to stop feeling before they have processed what happened. “You are too sensitive” turns a conversation about impact into an attack on character. “I said I was sorry, what more do you want?” may communicate impatience rather than repair. Even advice can become a form of dismissal when it arrives before the person feels heard.

Another common mistake is forced positivity. Saying, “Everything happens for a reason,” or, “Just focus on the good,” may close down a painful conversation. People often need permission to acknowledge that something was unfair, disappointing, confusing, or frightening. Hope is valuable, but hope should not be used to erase reality. A more helpful response might be, “This is difficult, and I can see why you are struggling. We can think about the next step when you are ready.”

Validation is especially important during conflict, because conflict activates the urge to protect yourself. When accused, you may feel pressure to prove that you are a good person. You may interrupt, list the other person’s mistakes, or explain your intention repeatedly. But intention and impact are not identical. Your intention tells part of the story. The other person’s impact tells another part. Mature communication has room for both.

Consider a couple arguing about lateness. One partner says, “You do not respect my time.” The other immediately replies, “That is ridiculous. I am late once in a while, and you are always exaggerating.” The conversation now contains a character judgment, a counterattack, and a debate about the word always. A validating approach might be, “When I arrive late, it feels to you as if your time does not matter to me. I can understand why repeated delays would build resentment. I want to talk about how we can handle plans more reliably.” That response does not confess to being disrespectful. It identifies the emotional meaning and moves toward a practical agreement.

Validation can be offered through body language as well as words. Face the person if that feels safe and culturally appropriate. Keep your attention on them. Avoid a mocking smile or an impatient sigh. Let your tone communicate that you are not waiting for an opportunity to win. Small signals of attention can make a difficult conversation less threatening. Of course, eye contact and touch are not universal requirements. Respect personal comfort, culture, neurodiversity, and the context of the relationship.

You can also validate yourself. Self-validation means recognising your own feelings without treating every feeling as a command or a fact. You might say, “I am anxious because this uncertainty matters to me. Anxiety does not prove that something terrible is happening.” Or, “I am angry because I experienced that as disrespectful. I can decide how to respond without attacking.” Self-validation creates a pause between emotion and action. It allows you to care for yourself without demanding that another person immediately remove every uncomfortable feeling.

Here is a simple checklist you can use when someone shares an emotional experience. First, have I actually listened? Second, can I name what they may be feeling without mocking or minimising it? Third, have I acknowledged why that feeling makes sense in context? Fourth, have I checked whether my understanding is correct? Fifth, have I asked what kind of support they want? And finally, can I share my perspective without using it to cancel theirs?

Let us practise with a few examples. If someone says, “I am nervous about meeting your family,” you could say, “That makes sense. You want to make a good impression, and you do not know what to expect.” You do not have to say that the meeting will be perfect. You are simply recognising the understandable source of the nervousness.

If a coworker says, “I felt embarrassed when you corrected me in front of everyone,” you could say, “I can see why that public correction felt uncomfortable. I was focused on the mistake, but I should have handled the feedback privately.” You can then discuss the work issue without pretending the delivery had no impact.

If a partner says, “I need more reassurance,” you could say, “I hear that uncertainty has been difficult, and you want to feel secure with me. I care about that. Let us talk about what reassurance would be helpful and what would become unsustainable for both of us.” This recognises the need while leaving room for healthy limits.

If a friend says, “I am disappointed that you cannot come,” you could say, “I understand why you were hoping I would be there. This event matters to you, and my absence is disappointing.” You do not need to cancel your own plans simply because the friend feels let down. Their disappointment can be real, and your boundary can remain real.

The goal of validation is not to make every conversation comfortable. Sometimes being heard leads to an uncomfortable truth. Sometimes a person will still be angry after you validate them. Sometimes validation will reveal that the two of you want different things. That is okay. Validation is not a guarantee of agreement, forgiveness, or a particular outcome. It is a commitment to treat emotional experience as worthy of respectful attention.

There is also a limit to how much emotional labour one person can provide. You cannot be available every minute. You cannot validate someone while they insult you, manipulate you, or demand that you deny your own reality. You can say, “I want to understand you, but I need us to speak without insults. I am going to take a break and return to this conversation at seven.” A pause with a clear return is different from abandonment. It protects the possibility of a better conversation.

As you practise, pay attention to your own habits. Do you rush to reassure because another person’s distress makes you uncomfortable? Do you debate facts because feelings seem dangerous? Do you apologise automatically, even when you need more information? Do you use validation to avoid setting limits? Awareness helps you find the balance between empathy and self-respect.

The next time someone shares a feeling, resist the urge to immediately correct, compare, advise, or defend. Try three sentences instead. “I hear that this affected you.” “I can understand why it felt that way.” “I would like to understand more before we decide what comes next.” These words do not require you to abandon your truth. They create enough room for two truths to be explored carefully.

Remember, validation is not agreement. It is not surrender. It is not approval of every behaviour or belief. It is a form of relational accuracy: the honest recognition that another person is having an emotional experience, and that the experience deserves to be understood before it is judged. When people feel heard, they are often better able to hear you too.

Today, share this with someone who needs it, and consider practising one validating sentence in a real conversation. Listen for the feeling beneath the complaint. Reflect what you heard. Acknowledge why it makes sense. Then, when the moment is calmer, share your own view with honesty and care. That is how disagreement can become connection instead of combat.
