# Glossary

**Actor-observer difference** — The tendency to explain one’s own behavior through circumstances while explaining another person’s behavior through personal characteristics.

**Attachment** — A system through which people seek closeness, protection, and emotional regulation in important relationships.

**Attribution** — The process of explaining the causes of behavior or events.

**Automaticity** — The tendency for a repeated action to occur with little conscious effort in a familiar context.

**Availability heuristic** — Judging likelihood partly by how easily examples come to mind.

**Avoidance** — Reducing contact with a feared feeling, situation, memory, or decision.

**Bystander effect** — Reduced likelihood of intervention when other people are present, especially when responsibility is unclear.

**Cognitive dissonance** — Psychological discomfort that can occur when beliefs, attitudes, or behavior conflict.

**Cognitive load** — The amount of mental effort being used by working memory at a particular time.

**Confirmation bias** — The tendency to seek, notice, and interpret information in ways that support an existing belief.

**Conformity** — A change in behavior, judgment, or expression in response to real or imagined group pressure.

**Defensiveness** — A protective response to perceived threat, criticism, shame, or loss of control.

**Diffusion of responsibility** — The tendency for personal responsibility to feel reduced when it is shared across a group.

**Emotion regulation** — Processes used to influence the intensity, duration, expression, or meaning of an emotional response.

**Emotional flooding** — A state of high arousal in which listening, reasoning, and self-control may become more difficult.

**Extrinsic motivation** — Motivation shaped by external outcomes such as rewards, approval, money, or avoiding punishment.

**Fundamental attribution error** — The tendency to overemphasize personal characteristics when explaining another person’s behavior and underemphasize circumstances.

**Groupthink** — A group decision pattern in which pressure for cohesion interferes with critical evaluation.

**Heuristic** — A mental shortcut used to make a judgment or decision efficiently.

**Identity** — A person’s sense of continuity and distinctiveness, including roles, values, memories, relationships, and group memberships.

**Implicit bias** — Automatic associations that may influence judgment without conscious endorsement.

**In-group** — A group with which a person identifies or feels membership.

**Intermittent reinforcement** — A pattern in which a behavior is rewarded unpredictably, potentially making the behavior persistent.

**Intrinsic motivation** — Motivation arising from interest, meaning, satisfaction, or enjoyment in the activity itself.

**Loss aversion** — The tendency for losses to feel more powerful than equivalent gains.

**Moral exclusion** — Treating people or groups as outside the circle of those entitled to ordinary fairness and concern.

**Narrative identity** — The evolving life story through which a person organizes memory and understands the self.

**Normative influence** — Group influence driven by the desire for acceptance or avoidance of rejection.

**Observational learning** — Learning through watching models, actions, and consequences.

**Present bias** — The tendency to give disproportionate weight to immediate rewards and costs compared with future outcomes.

**Projection** — Attributing an unwanted feeling, motive, or quality in oneself to another person; a possible process, not a casual diagnosis.

**Psychological reactance** — Motivational resistance that can arise when a person feels their freedom of choice is being restricted.

**Reinforcement** — A consequence that increases the likelihood of a behavior recurring.

**Rumination** — Repetitive, often passive thinking about distress, problems, or their causes without effective problem-solving.

**Self-efficacy** — A person’s belief that they can perform actions needed to manage a task or situation.

**Self-esteem** — Evaluation of one’s worth, competence, belonging, and ability to cope.

**Self-serving bias** — The tendency to explain successes through personal qualities and failures through circumstances, although it varies by context.

**Social comparison** — Evaluating oneself in relation to other people.

**Social identity** — The part of self-concept derived from group memberships.

**Social learning** — Learning influenced by observation, interaction, consequences, and social models.

**Social norm** — A shared expectation about appropriate behavior within a group or culture.

**Status** — Social respect, prestige, influence, or position within a group.

**Stereotype** — A generalized belief about a social group.

**Stonewalling** — Withdrawing from interaction in a way that blocks communication; distinct from a communicated, time-limited regulating pause.

**Sunk-cost fallacy** — Continuing a choice because of resources already invested rather than evaluating future costs and benefits.

**Top-down processing** — Interpretation influenced by prior knowledge, expectations, and beliefs.

**Trait** — A relatively enduring tendency in behavior, emotion, or cognition.

**Validation** — Communicating that another person’s emotional experience is understandable in context without necessarily agreeing with every conclusion.

**Working memory** — The limited mental system used to hold and manipulate information briefly.

The manuscript uses these sources as conceptual foundations. Specific claims should be checked against the final typeset edition and expanded with additional primary sources where a chapter makes a detailed empirical claim. No source should be represented as proving more than it actually examined.

# Further Reading

- *The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat* by Oliver Sacks — humane neurological case writing and the relationship between brain, perception, and identity.
- *Influence* by Robert B. Cialdini — accessible discussion of social influence principles.
- *Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me)* by Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson — cognitive dissonance and self-justification.
- *The Righteous Mind* by Jonathan Haidt — moral intuition and group-based disagreement, read alongside critiques and current research.
- *Behave* by Robert M. Sapolsky — a broad, detailed account of biological, developmental, and social influences on behavior.
- *Attached* by Amir Levine and Rachel Heller — a popular introduction to adult attachment; readers should treat attachment categories as simplified and non-diagnostic.
- Publications and educational resources from the American Psychological Association and reputable university psychology departments.

# Author’s Note

I wrote this book to encourage curiosity before judgment. Psychology is powerful because it gives language to patterns that can otherwise feel confusing. It is also limited because no framework can contain the whole person.

Please use these ideas as invitations to observe, question, and communicate more carefully—not as labels to impose on strangers or weapons to win arguments. If a subject in this book connects with trauma, danger, persistent distress, or a mental-health concern, seek appropriate professional support. Understanding can be a beginning, but some situations require care beyond the page.
