# Part III — The Space Between People

# Chapter 9 — Why We Misunderstand Each Other

## The message after the message

A woman sends a brief reply: “Okay.” She intends to acknowledge the plan. Her friend reads it as disappointment. The friend becomes quiet. The woman notices the quiet and assumes the friend is angry. By the end of the day, both are reacting to a conversation neither of them deliberately had.

Misunderstanding is not always caused by poor vocabulary. People communicate through words, tone, timing, silence, facial expression, history, status, culture, and expectation. The listener does not receive a message like a parcel. They interpret it.

Communication therefore has at least two parts: what one person attempts to convey and what another person experiences. The gap between those parts is where many conflicts begin.

## Intent and impact

Intent answers, “What did I mean to do?” Impact answers, “What happened for the other person?” Both matter, but they answer different questions.

A joke may be intended as affection and experienced as humiliation. A request for space may be intended as regulation and experienced as abandonment. A correction may be intended to improve work and experienced as public disrespect.

Intent is useful context. It does not erase impact. Impact is important evidence. It does not automatically prove malicious intent. Mature communication makes room for both: “I did not intend to embarrass you, and I can see that correcting you publicly had that effect.”

This sentence is difficult because people often think that acknowledging impact means accepting a negative identity. It does not. It means accepting that actions enter other people’s experiences in ways we cannot fully control.

## The listener fills the gaps

Speech is incomplete. People leave out details, assume shared context, use shorthand, and rely on nonverbal cues. Listeners fill gaps with expectations. If trust is high, an ambiguous message may be interpreted generously. If trust is low, the same message may be interpreted as evidence of disregard.

This is why communication cannot be improved only by telling people to choose perfect words. The relationship and context influence what words mean.

A manager says, “We need to talk.” A new employee may imagine a dismissal. A trusted manager may create curiosity rather than panic. The words are the same; the history changes the emotional meaning.

When a message is ambiguous and the stakes are high, clarification is more efficient than guessing. “When you say we need to talk, should I prepare anything?” asks for information without accusing the speaker of creating fear.

## Listening to understand

Listening is not merely remaining silent while waiting for a turn. It is an attempt to understand content, emotion, meaning, and request.

Reflective listening is a method in which the listener summarizes what they heard and checks accuracy. “You are upset because the plan changed without warning, and you wanted a chance to prepare. Did I get that right?” The purpose is not to repeat every word. It is to show that the listener is trying to construct the other person’s meaning accurately.

Validation is part of listening but is not the same as agreement. “I can see why that felt dismissive” does not require “You were objectively dismissed.” The listener can recognize experience while continuing to investigate facts.

Listening also includes tolerating pauses. People sometimes need silence to find the sentence beneath the first sentence. Immediate advice can interrupt that discovery.

## The difference between direct and indirect communication

Direct communication states the message clearly. Indirect communication relies more heavily on context, implication, politeness, or shared understanding. Neither style is automatically superior. Directness can reduce ambiguity, but it can also feel harsh. Indirectness can preserve social harmony, but it can also create confusion.

Culture, power, age, gender expectations, personality, and relationship history influence communication style. A junior employee may communicate indirectly because direct disagreement feels risky. A family may use hints because open conflict has historically been punished. A person may become direct in writing and indirect in person.

The practical skill is flexibility. Ask, “What level of directness would make this conversation clearer and safer?” A respectful direct statement might be: “I cannot accept that deadline. I can deliver it on Friday.” A respectful indirect approach might begin with shared purpose: “I want to make sure the quality is strong; could we discuss the timeline?”

## Digital communication and missing tone

Text removes many cues: pace, volume, facial expression, timing, and physical presence. Short messages can be efficient, but they also invite projection. A period may look angry. A delay may look intentional. A joke may appear cruel. A long paragraph may feel like an attack before it is fully read.

When a digital conversation becomes emotionally complex, changing channels can help. A voice conversation restores tone. A video call restores some visual information. An in-person conversation may allow pauses and repair. But changing channels should not be used to pressure someone into a conversation they do not feel safe having.

A useful digital habit is to ask whether the message requires interpretation. If it does, add context: “I am not upset; I am replying quickly between meetings and will respond properly tonight.” Do not rely on endless clarification to manage another person’s anxiety, but recognize that small context can prevent avoidable confusion.

## Questions are not neutral

Questions can invite understanding, gather information, test loyalty, express criticism, or disguise accusation. “What were you thinking?” may be genuine curiosity or contempt depending on tone. “Are you sure?” may invite reflection or communicate that the speaker is not trusted.

Open questions leave room for the other person’s answer: “What mattered most to you?” “How did you experience that?” “What would help now?” Closed questions can be useful when specific information is needed, but repeated closed questions may feel like an interrogation.

Before asking, identify your purpose. Am I trying to understand, confirm a suspicion, make a point, or force a confession? Honest purpose improves the quality of the question.

## The conflict between impact and intention

Many arguments become stuck because each person is answering a different question. One person is describing impact; the other is defending intention. The first says, “That hurt me.” The second says, “I did not mean to hurt you.” Both statements can be true, but they do not meet.

A bridge sentence is: “I believe you did not intend that, and I want to understand how it affected you.” Another is: “I can see the impact, and I would like to explain the context without using it to erase what happened.”

This structure reduces the pressure to choose between innocence and empathy. A person can remain morally complex: not malicious, still responsible; not rejected, still hurt; not controlled, still affected.

## Misunderstanding and power

Communication occurs within power relationships. A boss can call a conversation “feedback,” but the employee may experience it as a threat because the boss controls pay or promotion. A parent can demand openness from a child while remaining unwilling to answer questions. A partner can describe monitoring as reassurance while the other person experiences coercion.

The same words do not carry the same weight when one person has greater power. Psychological insight must therefore include consent and safety. A communication technique should not be used to make a vulnerable person more accommodating to a powerful person’s demands.

When power is unequal, clarity includes the right to pause, refuse, seek support, or communicate in writing. Understanding someone’s feelings does not create an obligation to surrender privacy or autonomy.

## Repairing a misunderstanding

A repair conversation can follow six steps:

1. Describe the observable event.
2. Name the impact without assigning motive.
3. Ask whether your understanding is accurate.
4. Listen to the other person’s context.
5. Take responsibility for your part.
6. Agree on a future behavior or boundary.

For example: “When the plan changed and I heard about it from someone else, I felt excluded. I assumed you did not want me involved. Is that what happened? I want to hear your side. In the future, could you tell me directly when plans change?”

Repair does not require identical memories. It requires enough shared understanding to make a different next moment possible.

## Practical application

In close relationships, replace global claims with specific observations. At work, provide feedback privately when possible and describe the behavior, impact, and expectation. In families, do not force children to mediate adult conflict. Online, move high-stakes discussions away from messages that are easy to misread. In leadership, ask whether people can disagree without punishment.

For self-awareness, keep a misunderstanding journal. Record the message, your first interpretation, the emotion it created, the alternative explanations you considered, and what clarification revealed. Over time, patterns may appear: perhaps silence activates you, or directness feels threatening, or you tend to explain before listening.

## Reflection

- Do I focus more on my intention or the other person’s impact?
- What assumptions do I make when messages are brief or delayed?
- How does my communication change with people who have more or less power than I do?
- Do my questions invite answers or disguise accusations?
- When do I need a different communication channel?
- What would a good repair conversation sound like in a relationship I value?

## Chapter takeaways

- Communication is an interpretation process, not a simple transfer of words.
- Intent and impact are different forms of information.
- Trust and history influence how ambiguous messages are understood.
- Listening requires reflection, validation, curiosity, and tolerance for pauses.
- Direct and indirect styles each have strengths and risks.
- Digital communication removes cues and increases the need for context.
- Power and safety affect how messages are experienced.
- Repair becomes possible when people describe events, listen to context, own their part, and agree on a next step.
