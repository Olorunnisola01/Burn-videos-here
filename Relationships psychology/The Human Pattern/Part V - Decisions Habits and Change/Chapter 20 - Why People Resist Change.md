# Chapter 20 — Why People Resist Change

## The change everyone says they want

A team complains about an inefficient process for months. When leadership proposes a new system, the same people object. They list every possible problem, remember the old system’s advantages, and ask to delay. From the outside, their resistance looks irrational. From the inside, the old process is familiar, the new one is uncertain, and the cost of learning feels immediate.

People resist change for many reasons. They may fear loss, distrust the person proposing it, lack resources, protect identity, anticipate social consequences, or have learned that previous changes were temporary. Resistance can be a defense against improvement, but it can also be information about risk.

A person who resists change is not necessarily lazy or afraid of growth. A person who welcomes every new idea is not necessarily courageous. The meaning of resistance depends on the situation.

## Change contains both gain and loss

Even positive change involves loss. A promotion may bring opportunity and remove familiar routines. A child growing independent can be joyful and painful for a parent. Leaving a harmful relationship can create safety while also creating loneliness, financial uncertainty, or grief. A new habit may require giving up a familiar source of comfort.

People often resist because they are calculating the loss, not because they cannot see the benefit. If change is presented only as gain, the person may feel unseen. Acknowledging loss does not weaken the case for change. It makes the case more credible.

Ask, “What will this change make possible?” and “What will it make harder or unavailable?” Both questions belong in a serious decision.

## The status quo bias

Status quo bias is the tendency to prefer the current state of affairs, especially when alternatives are uncertain. Familiarity creates a sense of safety, even when the familiar arrangement is inefficient or painful.

The status quo has hidden advantages: people know the rules, relationships have adapted, and failure can be predicted. A new system reveals gaps in knowledge. People may be judged while learning. The current system may be bad but legible.

To reduce status quo bias, compare the proposed change with the real costs of staying the same. Do not compare a messy current reality with an idealized future. Compare the likely current path with the likely change path, including transition costs.

## Loss of competence

Change can make a capable person feel temporarily incompetent. A veteran worker may become a beginner again. A parent may face a new developmental stage. A person who leaves a familiar social role may not know how to behave in the new one.

This temporary loss of competence can feel like identity loss. The person is not only learning a task; they are learning who they are in a changed environment. Patience and practice matter. Training should allow questions and mistakes without public humiliation.

A person may resist because they are protecting dignity. Leaders who interpret every question as disloyalty make learning more difficult.

## Trust determines how change is received

The same change can be welcomed from one leader and rejected from another. People ask whether the person proposing change understands the consequences, shares the burden, and will remain present after implementation.

Past experience matters. If leaders have announced changes and abandoned them, employees may wait before investing energy. If a family member has promised change repeatedly without follow-through, skepticism may be reasonable.

Trust grows when change is explained honestly, risks are acknowledged, feedback has consequences, and leaders model the new behavior. “This will be easy” is often less reassuring than “This will be difficult in these ways, and here is how we will support the transition.”

## Reactance and threatened freedom

Psychological reactance is a motivational response that can occur when people feel their freedom to choose is being restricted. A teenager refuses a reasonable request because it is delivered as domination. An employee resists a policy because the process allowed no voice. A partner becomes more determined to do something after being ordered not to.

Reactance does not prove that the requested behavior is wrong. It signals that autonomy has become part of the conflict. Offering meaningful choices, explaining reasons, and distinguishing non-negotiable safety requirements from flexible preferences can reduce unnecessary resistance.

People are more likely to support change when they can participate in shaping how it occurs.

## Ambivalence: wanting two things

Ambivalence means holding conflicting feelings or motivations at the same time. A person may want to stop drinking and want the relief drinking provides. They may want to leave a job and fear losing stability. They may want intimacy and fear dependence.

Ambivalence is not necessarily indecision to be crushed. It is information about competing values and costs. Motivational interviewing, associated with William Miller and Stephen Rollnick, treats ambivalence as something to explore rather than defeat through argument. The approach emphasizes collaboration, respect for autonomy, and helping people articulate their own reasons for change.

A useful question is, “What do you like about the current pattern, and what do you dislike?” Then ask, “If nothing changed for a year, what would concern you? If change became possible, what would you hope to gain?”

## The stages of readiness

People vary in readiness. Someone may not yet see a problem, may be considering change, may be preparing, may be acting, or may be maintaining a new pattern. A person who is not ready for action may need information or honest reflection rather than a detailed plan.

Stages are not a rigid staircase. People move forward, backward, and sideways. A setback can produce learning. Pressure may create public compliance without private commitment.

Matching support to readiness improves communication. Ask what the person wants rather than assuming they want a lecture, a plan, or rescue.

## Change and relationships

When one person changes, relationships change around them. A family may resist a member’s new boundary because the old arrangement benefited others. Friends may feel threatened when someone stops drinking, becomes more ambitious, or develops different values. A partner may welcome growth and fear becoming less needed.

Healthy relationships renegotiate. They ask what remains, what changes, and what support is needed. Unhealthy systems may punish the person for breaking the role assigned to them.

Change does not require abandoning everyone. It may require explaining less, repeating a boundary, finding new communities, or accepting that some relationships were organized around the old behavior.

## How to make change more likely

Make the reason concrete. People rarely change because an abstract future is theoretically better. They change when the future connects to values, pain, responsibility, identity, or hope.

Make the first step small. A huge plan creates a large emotional threat. A small action produces evidence that change is possible.

Make progress visible. Track behavior, not only outcomes. Create feedback that is quick and specific.

Plan for friction. Identify the moments most likely to trigger a return to the old pattern. Decide in advance what support or alternative will be available.

Protect dignity. Shame may force temporary compliance but often weakens honest engagement. Accountability works better when the person can face reality without being treated as disposable.

Review and adapt. A plan that fails in practice may need redesign, not moral condemnation.

## When resistance is wise

Resistance can protect against manipulation, unsafe speed, exploitation, or poorly designed change. A person should ask who benefits, who carries the cost, what evidence supports the plan, and whether refusal is possible.

A company may call layoffs a necessary transformation while hiding executive decisions that created the crisis. A partner may call surveillance a new trust system. A leader may call dissent resistance to progress. The language of change does not guarantee the change is good.

Discernment asks whether the proposed change is aligned with values, fair in distribution, supported by evidence, and open to correction.

## Practical application

For personal habits, identify the reward the old behavior provides and design a replacement. At work, involve people who will use the new system in its design. In families, distinguish a boundary from a demand that everyone approve your growth. In leadership, communicate losses as well as benefits.

For self-reflection, choose one change you resist. Write the hoped-for gain, feared loss, identity threat, trust concern, and smallest safe experiment. Then decide what evidence would make you more willing to continue or stop.

## Reflection

- What loss does this change represent to me?
- Am I resisting because of risk, unfamiliarity, distrust, or threatened autonomy?
- What identity would I need to revise?
- Which part of me wants change, and which part wants protection?
- Is the proposed change genuinely fair and evidence-based?
- What small experiment could produce useful information?
- How can I protect dignity while remaining accountable?

## Chapter takeaways

- Resistance may reflect fear, loss, mistrust, identity threat, or useful caution.
- Change contains both gains and losses.
- Familiar systems feel safer because they are predictable, not necessarily because they are good.
- Temporary incompetence can feel like identity loss.
- Autonomy influences whether people accept or reject change.
- Ambivalence is information about competing motives.
- Support should match readiness rather than assume it.
- Sustainable change is specific, gradual, supported, measurable, and open to revision.
